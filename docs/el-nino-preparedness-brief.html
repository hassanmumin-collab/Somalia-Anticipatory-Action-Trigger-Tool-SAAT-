Executive summary

The Pacific has entered a strong El Niño. The Oceanic Niño Index reached +1.39 °C in June 2026 and continues to rise, and the Indian Ocean Dipole is weakly positive with further strengthening expected for the season. In the Horn of Africa this configuration enhances the October–December Deyr rains rather than suppressing them, which is the reverse of its effect in southern and eastern Africa. ICPAC's seasonal outlook indicates wetter-than-normal conditions across the Greater Horn and names 1997 and 2023 as reference analogue years.

Flooding along the Shabelle and Juba begins with rain in the Ethiopian highlands and reaches the Somali gauge towns four to six days later. In the 2019 and 2020 flood seasons, which are the cleanest recent flood analogues, displacement ran between roughly 468,000 and 547,000 people over three months, and it concentrated on a small number of receiving towns led by Belet Weyne, Baardheere, Balcad and Mogadishu. The interactive scenario in Section 5 places a planning central case for the 2026 Deyr near 585,000 people, within a range of roughly 515,000 to 655,000 once an asset-depletion adjustment is applied. At a 25 US dollar monthly transfer over three months to 80 % of the caseload, the implied first-response cash requirement is near 35 million US dollars, five times the current pipeline.

The economic exposure is dominated by livestock rather than crops. A disease outbreak that triggers a Gulf import ban carries a conditional loss of 51 to 328 million US dollars across the scenarios in Section 6, against a direct crop loss of 11 to 80 million. Set against an anticipatory financing pipeline currently sized near seven million dollars, the case for pre-positioning finance is clear.

Most of the actions that reduce a flood caseload have lead times of weeks to months and cannot be recovered once their window closes. The Tier 0 window covering vaccination, pre-positioning and canal repair is closing now. The decisions listed in Section 9 should be settled before the Deyr rains begin in October.

01The forecast and the premise

In the Horn of Africa, El Niño enhances the short rains

El Niño does not have one effect on rainfall. It has a different effect in each region, because it shifts the tropical circulation and the position of the rain belts. Across southern Africa, Indonesia and northern Australia it typically brings drought. Across the equatorial Pacific, the southern United States, coastal Peru, and the Horn of Africa it typically brings above-normal rain. Somalia sits in the wet half of that pattern.

The recent record is consistent with this. Somalia's 2020–2023 near-famine developed during a prolonged La Niña that produced five consecutive failed seasons. The severe flooding at Belet Weyne, Luuq and Jowhar in late 2023 developed during an El Niño with a positive Indian Ocean Dipole. Both regimes are visible in the ocean record, and the 2026 rise is steeper than the one that preceded the 2019 Deyr floods.

02Exposure geography

Three distinct hazards, in three parts of the country

Flood risk in Somalia is not uniform, and the anticipatory response differs by zone. The largest and most predictable exposure is riverine. The Shabelle passes through Belet Weyne, Bulo Burti, Jalalaqsi, Jowhar, Balcad and Afgooye; the Juba passes through Doolow, Luuq, Baardheere, Bu'aale and Jamaame before reaching the Indian Ocean at Kismayo. These towns and the irrigated land around them flood when the rivers exceed their high-risk levels, which is the trigger the existing SoDMA framework already monitors.

The second exposure is agropastoral. FEWS NET identifies the Bay, Bakool, Gedo and Hiiraan agropastoral areas as carrying a credible risk of Famine, IPC Phase 5, under extreme widespread flooding between October 2026 and January 2027. These areas combine rain-fed cropping, a depleted asset base and limited road access when the rains arrive.

The third exposure is northern flash flooding. Togdheer, Sool, Sanaag, Awdal, Woqooyi Galbeed and Bari have no river gauge network. Flash flooding there is driven by short, intense local rainfall on crusted ground, and it is not covered by the Tier 3 gauge trigger. This zone requires a separate rainfall-threshold trigger calibrated on impact records.

03Why the response will exceed the historical relationship

Four failed seasons changed the ground and the households

Standard planning fits the response to a flood using elasticities estimated from the past decade. For 2026 that approach understates the likely impact, through two mechanisms.

The physical mechanism

Soils desiccated by four failed seasons form a crust with reduced infiltration capacity. The same rainfall generates more surface runoff than it would on a normally wetted profile, which produces faster flash flooding in the rain-fed zones and quicker river rises downstream. The curve-number treatment used for semi-arid crusted soils inverts the standard assumption that dry ground absorbs more water. This inversion is physically motivated and consistent with observed 2023 behaviour, though it has not been calibrated specifically for Somalia.

The behavioural mechanism

Households that have sold their herds and exhausted their grain stores have no buffer against a shock. They displace at a smaller trigger, and earlier in the event, than the historical record implies. A displacement model fitted on stationary historical elasticities will therefore under-predict both the size and the speed of the 2026 caseload.

One Deyr flood in a normal year is a shock. The same flood after four failed seasons falls on a population with nothing left to sell.

04The lead time already in the system

The rivers rise days after the rain falls in Ethiopia

The gauge towns on the Shabelle and Juba flood from rain in the Ethiopian highlands rather than from local rain. The travel time from the highland catchments to the gauges is approximately four days on the Shabelle and six days on the Juba. That lag is the single largest source of usable warning in the system, and it is the element most commonly omitted from Somalia flood models. A model driven on local rainfall over Hiiraan detects the flood as it arrives rather than while it is still upstream.

Analysis of the 2023 sequence supports this. Monthly rainfall over the upstream Shabelle catchment ran roughly 87 mm above its seasonal average in the month before the river peaked at Belet Weyne and drove the displacement. Local rainfall at Belet Weyne in the same period was modest. The practical requirement is that the sub-seasonal and action triggers ingest an upstream catchment rainfall index, lagged and accumulated to the routing delay. The catchment definitions currently in the tool are placeholder bounding boxes and should be replaced with hydrological basin polygons before operational use.

05Displacement outlook for the 2026 Deyr

Sizing the caseload, the timing, and the money

This is the operative section for anticipatory financing. A model-based point forecast is not yet reliable, because the displacement generation model built for this analysis ranks flood risk better than a persistence baseline but does not yet beat it on event discrimination. The planning approach here is analogue-based, using the district-level displacement recorded in recent flood seasons, scaled for magnitude and for asset depletion.

The two cleanest flood analogues are the 2019 Deyr, where 88 % of the displacement was attributed to flooding, and the 2020 Gu, where the share was 85 %. The 2019 Deyr moved approximately 468,000 people, concentrated in October. The 2020 Gu moved approximately 547,000, concentrated in April and May. The 2023 sequence moved approximately 826,000, but over a period that mixed flood, drought and conflict displacement, so it sets an upper reference rather than a central one.

Site pressure. Belet Weyne alone takes 30 to 40 % of the riverine caseload in every analogue, and it floods at the same time. Doolow, Baardheere and the smaller Shabelle towns take arrivals that are large relative to their standing population. These are the sites where land, water and sanitation, and site management have to be secured in advance, because the ratio of arrivals to existing capacity cannot be managed reactively.

06Economic exposure

Four loss channels, and the largest is livestock, not crops

The economic cost of a Deyr flood runs through four channels. The first two are usually included in a first estimate. The third and fourth are usually omitted, and in 2023 the third was plausibly larger than direct crop loss. The figures below are scenario outputs computed by the model from the stated assumptions, none of which is calibrated to Somalia. They show the relative weight of the channels and the order of magnitude at stake.

1 · Direct crop loss
Inundated area multiplied by yield, price, and a loss fraction that depends on how long the crop is submerged. Maize and sesame suffer near-total loss beyond three to five days of submergence at the vegetative stage. Sorghum is more tolerant. Crop close to harvest is partly salvageable. The damage is driven by duration, not by depth.
2 · Livestock, via Rift Valley Fever and a Gulf import ban
Direct flood mortality in livestock is modest. The material channel is a disease outbreak that triggers an import suspension by Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Oman. Livestock is Somalia's largest export earner and the primary source of foreign exchange for Berbera and Bosaso. This is a low-probability, high-consequence event. The El Niño to Rift Valley Fever association rests on a small number of reference events, in 1997–98 and 2006–07, so the probability is poorly constrained.
3 · Second-order irrigation damage
Silted canals, breached embankments and barrage damage reduce the yield of the following season. This is the mechanism by which one Deyr flood produces two poor harvests. It determines whether the event is a one-season or a two-season shock, which is the single largest sensitivity in the economic picture.
4 · Recovery upside, on a separate time axis
Pasture regeneration, improved conception rates and herd rebuilding over 12 to 18 months. After four failed seasons this is substantial. It belongs on a separate time axis and should not be netted against the immediate caseload in headline figures. A downside-only estimate will be dismissed by anyone who works in pastoral livelihoods.

07The preparedness ladder

Four tiers, each with its own lead time, envelope and actions

Preparedness is a sequence of decisions taken at decreasing lead times. Each tier fires on a distinct indicator, releases a fixed share of the anticipatory envelope, and triggers actions agreed in advance. Tier 3 replicates the existing SoDMA and WFP river-gauge trigger without modification. The value added is the three readiness tiers around it and a caseload forecast that sizes the money to the event rather than to last year's actuals.

08The economics of early action

A trigger that is often wrong still costs less than waiting

The main objection to an early trigger is that forecasts are imperfect and funds will sometimes be released for a flood that does not arrive. The relevant comparison is not the trigger against a perfect forecast. It is the trigger against the two alternatives available in practice: releasing funds every season regardless, or waiting until the flood is confirmed. When the action is inexpensive relative to the loss it prevents, acting on an imperfect trigger is cheaper than either alternative.

What is modelled and what is assumed

The cost-loss decision model, the trigger tier logic, the catchment routing method and the displacement panel are implemented and validated on real data. The Section 5 scenario is analogue-based and is not a model point forecast; the generation model does not yet beat a persistence baseline on event discrimination. The Section 6 economic figures are scenario outputs computed by the model from stated illustrative assumptions, none of which is calibrated to Somalia. The maps are exposure schematics, not inundation models. Figure 1 is a schematic of the typical El Niño response and not a forecast for this event.

Status

This brief is decision support and not an operational instruction. The gauge thresholds, the cost and loss parameters and the loss magnitudes are all pending agreement by the working group. The underlying analysis tool, SAAT, and the interactive Trigger Economics companion carry the full method and its limitations.
