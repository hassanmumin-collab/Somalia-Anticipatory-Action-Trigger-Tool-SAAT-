Analysis · Climate risk and anticipatory action

The 2026 Deyr El Niño in Somalia: A Historical-Analogue Impact Assessment Scaled From the 2023 Floods, With a Mogadishu Urban Drainage Case Study

Hassan Mumin

17 September 2026

Abstract

This analysis develops an order-of-magnitude assessment of the likely humanitarian and economic impact of the 2026 Deyr rains on Somalia, using a historical-analogue method rather than a mechanistic hazard model. Sea-surface temperature and Indian Ocean Dipole indices place the current El Niño event on a trajectory that, by the World Meteorological Organization's own median forecast, exceeds the peak strength of the event that produced Somalia's 2023 Deyr floods — the most recent, most thoroughly documented flood disaster in the country, with an independently assessed toll of 188 deaths, $176.0 million in direct damage and losses, and approximately 418,000 people displaced by flooding specifically. Scaling that documented baseline by the ratio of forecast-to-observed peak ocean strength (1.3) yields planning estimates of approximately 543,000 people displaced, 244 deaths, and $228.8 million in damage and lost economic activity nationally for the 2026 Deyr season. A case study of Mogadishu's urban drainage system, drawing on a September 2026 rainfall event and a documented May 2025 flash flood, indicates that the binding constraint on urban flood risk in the capital is drainage capacity relative to short-duration rainfall intensity, rather than seasonal rainfall totals: approximately 5 mm of rainfall was sufficient to flood major arterial roads, while a single 2025 storm delivering on the order of 115 mm in eight hours killed at least seven people. The analysis closes with four preparedness recommendations addressing municipal drainage maintenance, household-level financial resilience among daily-wage workers, riverine early-warning operations, and pre-positioned agricultural recovery inputs, and discusses the material limitations of the scaling method in detail.

Keywords: El Niño–Southern Oscillation; Indian Ocean Dipole; Somalia; Deyr rains; flood risk; anticipatory action; urban drainage; historical-analogue forecasting

Introduction

Somalia's most severe flood disasters have historically coincided with El Niño events accompanied by a positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), a combination that enhances, rather than suppresses, the October–December Deyr rains over the Horn of Africa.[1,4] The 1997–98 event, associated with more than 2,000 flood-related deaths in Somalia and regional rainfall totals five to ten times the seasonal norm, remains the principal historical reference point for catastrophic Deyr flooding in the country. The most recent comparable event occurred in 2023, when a moderate El Niño combined with an extreme positive IOD — one of only five such events identified in the post-1980 instrumental record[9] — produced flooding that killed 188 people and caused $176.0 million in documented damage and losses.[5,6]

As of September 2026, oceanic and atmospheric indices indicate that the current El Niño event is on a trajectory to exceed the 2023 event in strength, and by some measures may approach the strongest event in the instrumental record of the relevant index.[1,2] This raises an immediate, practical question for humanitarian and municipal planning: in the absence of a validated, Somalia-specific hazard-impact model, what is a defensible, evidence-based estimate of the scale of impact to prepare for?

This analysis addresses that question with a historical-analogue method: rather than model flood impact from first principles — which would require calibrated exposure, vulnerability and drainage-capacity data that does not yet exist for Somalia at the necessary resolution — the 2023 Deyr floods are treated as an empirical baseline and scaled by the ratio of this event's forecast peak ocean strength to 2023's confirmed peak. Section 2 sets out this method and its data sources in full. Sections 3–4 place the 2026 event in its climatic and geographic context. Section 5 develops a focused case study of Mogadishu's urban flood risk, motivated by the city's documented sensitivity to comparatively small rainfall totals. Sections 6–8 present the scaled displacement, mortality and economic-loss estimates. Section 9 sets out preparedness recommendations, Section 10 discusses the method's limitations, and Section 11 concludes.

Data and methods

2.1 Oceanic and climatic indices

Pacific sea-surface temperature anomalies are reported using the weekly Niño 3.4 index (NOAA Climate Prediction Center / Physical Sciences Laboratory) and the associated Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), a three-month running mean of the same quantity.[2] NOAA adopted a revised, relative ONI (RONI) standard in February 2026, which changes the ranking of historical events relative to the traditional ONI methodology used for the multi-decade comparison in Section 3; we report traditional-method values for historical events throughout and flag this methodological transition explicitly rather than mix methodologies silently. Indian Ocean conditions are reported using the Dipole Mode Index (DMI).[3]

2.2 Historical baseline

We use the 2023 Deyr floods (October–December 2023) as the empirical baseline for this assessment because it is the most recent Somalia flood event for which an independent, multi-agency Post-Disaster Needs Assessment (PDNA) exists, covering mortality, displacement and sectoral economic loss,[5] cross-checked against contemporaneous OCHA situation reporting[7] and FEWS NET/FSNAU agricultural field assessments.[8] The baseline figures used are: 188 confirmed deaths; $176.0 million in total documented damage ($126.6 million) and losses ($49.5 million); and, for displacement specifically, 418,000 people displaced by flooding once concurrent drought- and conflict-driven displacement is excluded from the roughly 826,000 total recorded movements associated with the same reference period. We note that the PDNA itself reports a larger displacement figure — over 2 million — using a broader, cumulative definition of displacement across the 2023 season and multiple hazards.[5,6] We use the narrower, flood-attributed figure for consistency with the flood-specific scope of this analysis, and record this discrepancy explicitly here rather than resolve it by selecting whichever figure is more convenient for the argument.

2.3 Scaling procedure

The scaling factor applied to the 2023 baseline is the ratio of the World Meteorological Organization's median forecast for this event's October–December 2026 peak Niño 3.4 anomaly (+2.67 °C)[1] to 2023–24's confirmed peak (+2.0 °C, November–January)[2]: a factor of 1.3. This factor is applied uniformly to the displacement, mortality and economic-loss baselines described above. This is a deliberately simple method, and Section 10 discusses its limitations at length; it is preferred here to a more elaborate model because Somalia does not currently have the calibrated exposure, vulnerability and drainage-capacity data that a more mechanistic estimate would require, and a simple, transparent, falsifiable method is preferable to a more complex one whose additional apparent precision the underlying data cannot support.

2.4 Mogadishu case study data

Mogadishu-specific rainfall figures are drawn from published long-run climate normals,[13] local and press reporting on a 16 September 2026 rainfall event,[14] and a SoDMA field report on a 9 May 2025 flash flood,[10] cross-checked against independent press reporting on that event's death toll.[11] Bajaaj (motorised three-wheeler) driver population figures are drawn from Benadir Regional Administration registration data and published fieldwork;[12] we treat this as an approximate, dated estimate rather than a current census figure, and state that limitation explicitly wherever the figure is used.

Climatic context: the 2026 Deyr in historical perspective

For the week of 9 September 2026, the Niño 3.4 anomaly reached +2.9 °C, the highest reading of this event to date, the warmest September week in this index since 1981, and within 0.1 °C of the all-time weekly maximum of +3.0 °C recorded on 18 November 2015.[2] The WMO's multi-model forecast places the median October–December 2026 peak at +2.67 °C, assigns a 75 percent probability that this peak will exceed the previous record set in 1982–83, and now assigns a 98 percent probability to this event reaching "Super El Niño" status.[1] The WMO Secretary-General has characterised the event's current trajectory as potentially "off the charts," language not previously used in a WMO seasonal update.[1] For comparison, the 2023–24 event used as this analysis's baseline peaked at +2.0 °C.

The Indian Ocean Dipole is at least as consequential for Somalia as Pacific conditions, and often more so for the Deyr specifically. The 2023 positive IOD has been classified in the peer-reviewed literature as one of only five "extreme positive IOD" events identified since the 1980s (1994, 1997, 2006, 2019 and 2023),[9] and it reinforced, rather than offset, that year's El Niño over the Horn of Africa. As of 13 September 2026, the DMI stood at +0.65 °C, already positive, with forecasters expecting a moderate-to-strong phase by October–December — developing on a timeline comparable to 2023's rather than lagging it.[3,4] We treat the IOD as developing in line with, rather than in excess of, 2023's trajectory in the scaling procedure above (Section 2.3); Section 10 discusses the consequence of this conservative choice.

Exposure geography

Somalia's flood exposure concentrates in two distinct settings with different physical mechanisms and different warning characteristics. Riverine flooding along the Shabelle and Juba corridors originates as rainfall in the Ethiopian highlands and reaches Somali gauge towns — led by Belet Weyne, Baardheere, Luuq and Jowhar — four to six days later, providing genuine operational lead time where the upstream-to-downstream data chain is maintained. Urban pluvial flooding in Mogadishu follows a different mechanism entirely: it is driven by local rainfall intensity relative to the capacity of an already-degraded stormwater network, with no equivalent lead time. A fuller geographic treatment, including satellite-derived inundation-history mapping of the river corridor, is provided in the companion ministerial brief on this event; the remainder of this analysis focuses in particular on the Mogadishu case, both because it is comparatively under-characterised in existing assessments and because of the disproportionate share of the city's population dependent on daily, cash-based income (Section 8).

Case study: rainfall intensity and drainage capacity in Mogadishu

Mogadishu's long-run climate normals are, by regional standards, comparatively modest: published datasets place average annual rainfall in the range of approximately 400–540 mm, depending on the period and dataset used, with May typically the wettest month.[13] We report this as a range rather than a single figure because published climate normals for Mogadishu vary appreciably across sources and reference periods, and presenting a single point estimate would overstate the precision actually available.

On 16 September 2026, Mogadishu received approximately 5 mm of rainfall. Within that range of published normals, this is not an atypical daily total for the rainy season. Nonetheless, major arterial roads — including the KM4 junction and the corridor to Aden Adde International Airport — were reported flooded, with vehicles stranded and traffic substantially disrupted.[14] This is consistent with independent reporting identifying these same locations as recurring flood points in prior rain events since 2023, attributable to silted, obstructed or otherwise non-functional drainage infrastructure rather than to rainfall volume as such.

The city's own recent record indicates the scale of rainfall intensity its drainage system cannot accommodate. On 9 May 2025, SoDMA's field report recorded rainfall of 95–120 mm over a two-to-ten-hour window;[10] a commonly cited figure for the same event, attributed to Somalia's Ministry of Energy and Water, specifies more than 115 mm in approximately eight hours. Independent press reporting places the resulting death toll at a minimum of seven, with some reports citing up to nine or ten as information was updated in the days following the event; we report "at least seven" as the figure supported by the earliest, most widely corroborated reporting, while noting the higher figures explicitly.[11] More than 200 families were displaced and several homes were destroyed across multiple districts, and key highways were damaged sufficiently to disrupt movement for days.[10,11] That single event delivered on the order of twenty-three times the rainfall recorded on 16 September 2026, within a broadly comparable time window.

Taken together, these two events indicate that the binding constraint on Mogadishu's urban flood risk is the interaction between short-duration rainfall intensity and drainage capacity, rather than the seasonal rainfall total. This is broadly consistent with the general characterisation of El Niño years in East Africa as producing not simply wetter seasons but a higher likelihood of intense, short-duration rainfall bursts; we note, however, that no official day-by-day rainfall forecast exists for the peak of the 2026 Deyr, and none is offered here. The two events in Figure 2 should be read as illustrative bounds on recently observed intensity, not as a forecast of what the 2026 Deyr will bring.

Displacement projection for the 2026 Deyr

Applying the 1.3× scaling factor (Section 2.3) to the 418,000 flood-attributable displacement baseline from 2023 yields a central estimate of approximately 543,000 people displaced during the 2026 Deyr, within an illustrative range of roughly 480,000 to 606,000 obtained by varying the multiplier by ±0.15 around its central value.[5,7]

Expected mortality

Applying the same procedure to the 188 confirmed deaths recorded in 2023 yields a central estimate of approximately 244 deaths for the 2026 Deyr, within a range of roughly 216 to 273. This estimate is a national total and does not, and cannot, indicate what share of mortality will occur in Mogadishu relative to riverine towns, because no such breakdown exists in the public record even for the 2023 baseline event.[5,6,7]

Economic loss and household-level exposure

Applying the same procedure to the $176.0 million baseline yields a central estimate of approximately $228.8 million in damage and lost economic activity nationally, within a range of roughly $202.4 million to $255.2 million.[5,6] In the 2023 baseline event, agriculture and livestock constituted the single largest recovery-investment sector, ahead of transport/roads and housing, consistent with FEWS NET/FSNAU field data recording approximately 83,360 hectares of cropland damaged nationally by flash and river floods (37,700 ha in Juba, 20,850 ha in Hiiraan, 17,260 ha in Bay, 4,700 ha in Bakool and 2,850 ha in Gedo), and separately, with cereal production in the hardest-hit districts — Gedo, Hiiraan and Middle Shabelle — reported 55 to 58 percent below the long-term mean.[8] Five towns — Beletweyn, Dolow, Baardhere, Baydhaba and Kismayo — accounted for approximately 70 percent of total documented losses and damages in 2023.[5,6]

These national totals understate the point for a city such as Mogadishu, where a large share of the population depends on daily, cash-in-hand income: petty trade, casual labour, market vending and transport. Such households typically hold no accumulated financial buffer, have no employer able to continue wages through a period of impassable roads, and have no remote-work substitute for a market stall or transport route that floodwater has closed. The clearest single illustration of this exposure is Mogadishu's fleet of an estimated 33,000–35,000 bajaajs (motorised three-wheelers), which move a large share of the capital's passengers and goods.[12] Most drivers do not own the vehicle they operate; a commonly reported rental arrangement has drivers paying approximately $15 per day and retaining the residual after fuel costs, a structure with negligible tolerance for a lost working day. We treat the driver-count figure as an approximate, dated estimate rather than a current census (Section 10).

Limitations

The scaling method used here is deliberately simple, and its limitations should be stated plainly rather than left implicit. First, it assumes that flood impact scales approximately linearly with peak ocean-index strength, which is a simplification of a substantially more complex relationship involving antecedent soil moisture, drainage-system condition at the time of the event, and the specific spatial distribution of rainfall — none of which is captured by a single strength ratio. Second, the same multiplier is applied uniformly across displacement, mortality and economic loss, despite no evidence that these three domains actually respond to ocean-index strength at the same rate; this uniformity is a modelling convenience, not an empirical finding. Third, the method offers no mechanism-level decomposition — it cannot indicate whether 2026's impact will concentrate in Mogadishu or in riverine towns, nor what share of mortality will be attributable to drowning as opposed to electrocution in the urban setting, because no such breakdown exists even for the 2023 baseline in the public record. Fourth, the IOD component of ocean forcing is treated as developing in line with, rather than in excess of, 2023's trajectory (Section 3); to the extent the IOD proves more extreme than 2023's, the estimates in Sections 6–8 will understate the likely impact. Fifth, this analysis credits none of the preparedness actions recommended in Section 9: if drainage clearance, early warning and pre-positioning referenced there are implemented, the realised 2026 outcome should fall below these estimates, not above them. Sixth, several figures used in the Mogadishu case study — the bajaaj driver count, and Mogadishu's climate normals — are approximate, dated, or drawn from a range of published values rather than a single authoritative source, and are reported as such throughout rather than with false precision. Finally, the displacement baseline used here (418,000, flood-attributed) differs materially from the PDNA's own broader displacement figure (over 2 million, cumulative across hazards and the full 2023 season); readers should treat the two figures as measuring different things rather than as competing estimates of the same quantity.

Taken together, the figures presented in Sections 6–8 should be read as an order-of-magnitude planning baseline derived transparently from a real, documented event and a published strength forecast — not as a prediction of where or how impact will occur in 2026.

Conclusion

Available oceanic and atmospheric evidence indicates that the 2026 Deyr is developing under conditions that already exceed, and are forecast to substantially exceed, the conditions that produced Somalia's 2023 Deyr floods — the country's most recent major flood disaster and the most thoroughly documented one available as an analytical baseline. Scaling that documented event by the ratio of forecast-to-observed peak ocean strength provides a transparent, if necessarily approximate, basis for anticipatory planning, yielding order-of-magnitude estimates of roughly 543,000 people displaced, 244 deaths and $228.8 million in economic loss nationally. Independent of that national-scale estimate, a Mogadishu-specific case study indicates that the city's urban drainage system is already failing at rainfall intensities well below what a strong Deyr season has recently produced elsewhere in the country, and that a substantial share of the capital's population — including an estimated 33,000–35,000 bajaaj drivers and the wider daily-cash-flow economy they represent — has limited structural capacity to absorb the resulting loss of working days. The four recommendations in Section 9 follow directly from these findings and are actionable on the time horizon remaining before the 2026 Deyr rains intensify.

Appendix · Data provenance and model assumptions

Core scaling assumptions

  • The 2026 scaling factor is fixed at 1.3×, calculated as the WMO's median forecast peak Niño 3.4 anomaly for October–December 2026 (+2.67 °C) divided by 2023–24's confirmed peak (+2.0 °C).
  • The same factor is applied uniformly to displacement, mortality and economic-loss baselines; this is a simplifying assumption, not a mechanistic model of how each domain actually responds to ocean-index strength.
  • The Indian Ocean Dipole is treated as developing in line with, not in excess of, 2023's trajectory; if it proves more extreme, the estimates in Sections 6–8 understate likely impact.
  • Illustrative ranges around each central estimate are obtained by varying the 1.3 multiplier by ±0.15; this range reflects sensitivity to the multiplier choice, not a statistically derived confidence interval.
  • No mechanism-level or geographic decomposition (Mogadishu vs. riverine; drowning vs. electrocution) is offered, because none exists in the public record for the 2023 baseline either.

Known data conflicts, disclosed rather than silently resolved

  • Displacement: the PDNA reports "over 2 million" displaced (broad, cumulative, multi-hazard, full 2023 season); this analysis instead uses 418,000 (flood-attributed only) for consistency with its flood-specific scope. These are not competing estimates of the same quantity.
