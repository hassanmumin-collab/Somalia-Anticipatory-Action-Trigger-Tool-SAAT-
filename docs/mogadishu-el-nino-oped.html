Analysis · Somalia & the 2026 Deyr rains

Five Millimetres, and Mogadishu Went Under

The ocean data says this is already a stronger El Niño than the one that flooded Belet Weyne and Baidoa in 2023. The capital just got a preview of what its drainage system can and cannot handle — and it failed the test on a day of ordinary rain.

Hassan Mumin · 17 September 2026

On the afternoon of 16 September 2026, Mogadishu received roughly five millimetres of rain — a passing shower by the standards of a coastal tropical city. By evening, cars sat stalled axle-deep at the KM4 junction and along the road to Aden Adde International Airport, arteries that carry much of the capital's daily traffic in and out of the city centre. Schools let out into standing water. It was not a flood in the sense that will make international news. It was something more useful to pay attention to: a demonstration, at low cost, of how little margin this city's drainage system has left before the season that is coming.

Because the season that is coming is not an ordinary Deyr. Sea-surface temperatures in the equatorial Pacific are tracking toward what the World Meteorological Organization has called a potential "off the charts" El Niño — language it does not use lightly, and has not used before in a seasonal update. Somalia's most recent point of comparison, the floods that displaced hundreds of thousands of people and killed at least 188 in late 2023, developed under an El Niño that this year's event has already surpassed, months before its own peak is even forecast to arrive.

What the ocean is telling us

El Niño is usually described by a single number: the Oceanic Niño Index, or ONI, which tracks how much warmer than average the surface of the central Pacific has become. For the week of 9 September 2026, that anomaly reached +2.9 °C — the highest reading of this event so far, and the warmest September week in the Niño 3.4 region since 1981. The WMO's own multi-model forecast puts the October–December seasonal peak at a median of +2.67 °C, gives a 75 percent chance that this event will exceed the previous record set in 1982–83, and now assigns a 98 percent probability to this officially becoming a "Super El Niño." For comparison, the 2023–24 event that produced the floods this piece keeps returning to peaked at +2.0 °C.

The Indian Ocean matters just as much for Somalia as the Pacific does, and often more for the Deyr specifically. The Indian Ocean Dipole was positive and unusually strong in 2023 — one of only five "extreme positive" IOD events recorded since the 1980s — and it reinforced rather than offset that year's El Niño over the Horn of Africa. This year's Dipole Mode Index stood at +0.65 °C as of 13 September, already in positive territory, and forecasters expect it to strengthen into a moderate-to-strong phase by October–December, developing on the same timeline as 2023 rather than lagging behind it. Two oceans, both pointing the same direction, at the same time of year, more forcefully than they did the last time this region flooded.

Why five millimetres can drown a city

It is tempting to read the ocean numbers and picture the risk arriving in October, in a single dramatic storm. That is not quite right, and it understates what is already true. The danger in a city like Mogadishu is not the seasonal total of rain; it is the mismatch between how fast rain can fall and how fast a city built without a functioning stormwater network can move that water out. Mogadishu's climate is, on paper, comparatively dry — on the order of 450 millimetres a year across most published climate normals, with May the wettest month at roughly 90 millimetres, or about three millimetres on an average day within the rainy season. Sixteen September's five millimetres was not, by that measure, an unusual day. It flooded major roads anyway, because the drainage channels that should carry that water to the sea are silted, built over, or blocked by solid waste at the very points — the KM4 junction and the Airport Road corridor chief among them — that flood on almost every significant rain event.

The city's own recent history sets the scale of what "significant" can mean. On 9 May 2025, a single storm dropped more than 115 millimetres of rain on Mogadishu in about eight hours. The drainage system, already overwhelmed hours in, could not cope: at least seven people died, more than 22,500 were affected, and key highways were damaged badly enough to disrupt movement for days. That is roughly twenty-three times sixteen September's rainfall, arriving in a similarly short window. It is also well within the range of what a single intense Deyr storm can produce once an El Niño this strong is fully established, since El Niño's primary effect on East African rainfall is not simply "more days of rain" but a higher likelihood of the short, intense bursts that overwhelm urban drainage in hours rather than days.

The question this September's small storm answers is not "will Mogadishu flood this Deyr." It already has, on a day of unremarkable rain. The question is how many multiples of that the city's arteries will have to absorb once the rains this El Niño is forecast to bring actually arrive.

What 2023 already showed us, scaled up

Somalia does not have to guess what a strong El Niño Deyr looks like in human and economic terms, because it lived through one two years ago. The floods of October–December 2023 — concentrated on Belet Weyne, Luuq, Jowhar and the riverine towns of the Shabelle and Juba — killed a confirmed 188 people and caused an assessed $176 million in direct damage and losses, according to the joint Rapid Post-Disaster Needs Assessment carried out by the Somali Disaster Management Agency with the United Nations, the World Bank and the European Union. Agriculture and livestock absorbed the single largest share of that damage; roughly 83,000 hectares of crops were lost nationally, and cereal production in the hardest-hit districts came in more than half below the long-run average. Displacement from that sequence totalled some 418,000 people once drought- and conflict-related movements are excluded.

If this year's event is, by the ocean measures above, running at roughly 1.3 times the strength of 2023's — the ratio between WMO's forecast peak for this October–December and 2023's confirmed peak — then scaling 2023's documented toll by that same factor gives a rough, order-of-magnitude sense of what is now in play for the 2026 Deyr: on the order of 540,000 people displaced, roughly 240 deaths, and something over $220 million in damage and lost economic activity nationally.

The economy that cannot work from home

National totals understate the point for a city like Mogadishu, because so much of its economy runs on people who have to physically move through it every day to earn anything at all. A large share of Mogadishu's households depend on daily, cash-in-hand income: petty trade, casual labour, market vending, transport. There is no accumulated buffer in most of these households, no employer continuing a salary through a week of impassable roads, no remote-work substitute for a market stall that floodwater has closed.

The clearest single illustration of that exposure is Mogadishu's fleet of an estimated 33,000–35,000 bajaajs — the three-wheeled auto-rickshaws that move a large share of the capital's passengers and goods. Most drivers do not own the vehicle they operate; they rent it for roughly $15 a day and keep whatever is left after fuel. That is a business model with no slack in it. A day when the Airport Road corridor, Bakaara Market approaches, or the KM4 junction are underwater is not a lost commute for a bajaaj driver; it is a lost day of income, full stop, with the daily rental cost still due. Multiply that by the thousands of small shopkeepers, porters and casual labourers whose earnings depend equally on the city's roads staying open, and a flood's economic damage is not confined to the crop and infrastructure losses that show up in a post-disaster assessment. It shows up first, and hardest, in households that were already living day to day.

What should happen before October

1 Clear the drains at the points that already fail every time

Benadir Regional Administration & Mogadishu Municipality

The city does not need to guess where its drainage infrastructure will fail this Deyr — it already knows. The KM4 junction and the Airport Road (Aden Adde) corridor have now flooded repeatedly across at least three separate rain events since 2023, including on 16 September with only about five millimetres of rain. A systematic clearance of existing culverts, channels and choke points at these known locations, completed before the rains intensify in October, is the single highest-return action available to the municipality: it does not require new infrastructure, new financing instruments or new institutions, only the political will to prioritise maintenance on a known list of failure points before they fail again at a much higher rainfall intensity.

2 Households and daily-wage workers should build a buffer now

Mogadishu households, particularly daily-cash-flow workers

For the roughly 33,000 bajaaj drivers, and for the far larger number of households who depend on a market stall, a construction job, or casual trade for that day's food, the rational response to this forecast is to build whatever buffer is possible before the rains peak: a few days of dry food staples, a small cash reserve set aside specifically against lost income days, and a known fallback route or income source for the days a primary road or market is underwater. This is not a call for households to absorb a risk that should be managed at the city level — it is a recognition that infrastructure fixes will not be complete by October, and that the households with the least slack in their daily budget are the ones most exposed to a single blocked road.

3 Give riverine towns the warning time the geography already provides

SoDMA, FAO SWALIM & upstream-downstream coordination with Ethiopia

Unlike Mogadishu's flash floods, riverine flooding on the Shabelle reaches towns like Belet Weyne four to six days after rain falls in the Ethiopian highlands upstream — real lead time, if the gauge and rainfall data are watched and acted on in real time. That lead time has already been shown, in the 2023 record, to reduce loss of life substantially where it is used. The priority between now and the Deyr peak is operational, not technical: keeping the upstream-to-downstream data chain live and ensuring the warning reaches the household level in the specific towns — led by Belet Weyne and Baardheere — that absorbed the largest share of both displacement and losses in 2023.

4 Move agricultural recovery inputs into riverine areas ahead of the rains, not after

FAO, Ministry of Agriculture & humanitarian partners

The single largest documented loss category in 2023 was agriculture and livestock, and the damage compounds if flooded canals and breached embankments are not repaired before the next planting cycle — one flood becomes two bad seasons instead of one. Pre-positioning seed, repair materials and cash for canal and embankment maintenance in the Shabelle and Juba corridors now, rather than after flooding is confirmed, is the difference between an agricultural sector that recovers within a season and one that does not.

How this analysis was done

The approach here follows a simple, deliberately conservative logic rather than a novel prediction model. Somalia's 2023 Deyr floods are the most recent, most thoroughly documented flood disaster in the country, with an independently assessed death toll, displacement figure and economic loss total. Rather than build a fresh forecast of 2026's impact from first principles — which would require calibrated data this country does not yet have, on everything from urban drainage capacity to household income buffers — this analysis scales that one confirmed, real event by a single factor: how much stronger the ocean conditions driving this year's Deyr are, relative to the ocean conditions that produced 2023's floods. That ratio, 1.3, comes directly from the World Meteorological Organization's own published forecast for this event's peak strength, divided by 2023's confirmed peak.

This method is intentionally simple, and its limits are real. It assumes flood impact scales roughly linearly with ocean strength, which is a simplification of a much more complicated relationship involving antecedent soil moisture, drainage conditions, and where exactly the heaviest rain falls. It does not, and cannot, tell you whether the deaths or losses in 2026 will concentrate in Mogadishu or in the river towns, because no such breakdown exists in the public record even for 2023. It also does not credit any of the preparedness action recommended above — if the drainage clearance, early warning and pre-positioning described here actually happen, the real 2026 outcome should come in below this estimate, not above it. The number is a planning baseline, not a prophecy.

Model assumptions and data sources

Core method

  • 2023 Deyr floods are treated as the reference event: 188 confirmed deaths; $176.0M in direct damage ($126.6M) and losses ($49.5M); ~418,000 people displaced by flooding specifically (drought- and conflict-displacement excluded from that figure).
  • The 2026 scaling factor is fixed at 1.3×, calculated as the WMO's median forecast peak Niño 3.4 anomaly for October–December 2026 (+2.67 °C) divided by 2023–24's confirmed peak (+2.0 °C, NDJ 2023–24).
  • The same factor is applied uniformly to displacement, deaths and economic loss. This is a simplifying assumption, not a mechanistic model of how each impact channel actually responds to ocean strength.
  • The Indian Ocean Dipole, currently developing on a timeline comparable to 2023's but not yet confirmed to exceed it, is treated as in line with 2023 rather than adding further to the multiplier. If the IOD also proves more extreme than 2023's, the estimates in this piece understate the likely impact.
  • Rainfall comparisons (16 September 2026; 9 May 2025) are individual storm reports, not a systematic rainfall record for the season, and there is no official day-by-day rainfall forecast for the peak of this El Niño; none is implied here.
  • All 2026 figures are national order-of-magnitude estimates, not predictions for any specific city, district or household, and carry no confidence interval beyond the approximate nature of the scaling method itself.
