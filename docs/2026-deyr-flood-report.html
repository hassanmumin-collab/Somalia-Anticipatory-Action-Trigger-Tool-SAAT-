Report · Somalia flood risk and anticipatory action

Somalia's 2026 Deyr Flood Risk: A Historical-Analogue Assessment

Hassan Mumin

17 September 2026

Summary

Somalia's five most severe Deyr-season floods since 1997 show that flood severity tracks the combined state of the Pacific (El Niño / ONI) and the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) — not El Niño strength on its own. The clearest evidence is 2015-16: the strongest El Niño on record at the time (ONI +2.6) produced a comparatively contained Somalia flood season, because that year's positive IOD was weak and ended before the Deyr peak. By contrast, 2019 had only a weak, borderline El Niño (ONI +0.5) but one of the most extreme positive IOD events on record, and produced severe flooding (3.4 million people affected).

As of 17 September 2026, the Pacific is on a near-record or record trajectory (weekly Niño 3.4 already at +2.9 °C). The Indian Ocean Dipole, however, is so far only weakly positive and merely forecast — not yet confirmed — to strengthen. That is the genuine open question this report cannot resolve in advance, and it is reported as a question rather than papered over with a single number. Section 5 gives two historically grounded ranges, one for each way the IOD could resolve, plus a probability-weighted expected value for displacement across a range of illustrative probability weights, since no forecasting centre publishes a precise probability that the IOD will reach an extreme-positive state specifically. For anticipatory-action sizing, this report recommends a single conservative planning floor instead: the 2023 baseline scaled by this event's ENSO strength ratio alone (1.3×) — 244 deaths, 543,128 displaced, $228.8M in damage and losses — a figure that deliberately exceeds every historical extreme-IOD event and every expected-value scenario in this report (Section 5.2).

1. The current forecast

For the week of 9 September 2026, the Niño 3.4 sea-surface temperature anomaly reached +2.9 °C — the highest reading of this event to date and the warmest September week in this index since 1981.[1] The World Meteorological Organization's multi-model forecast places the median October–December 2026 peak at +2.67 °C, with a 75 percent probability of exceeding the previous record set in 1982–83 and a 98 percent probability of this event reaching "Super El Niño" status.[1] The WMO Secretary-General has described the event's trajectory as potentially "off the charts."[1]

The Indian Ocean Dipole, as of 13 September 2026, stood at +0.65 °C — positive, but well short of the "extreme" classification carried by every one of the four most severe Deyr floods in the historical record below. Forecasters expect it to strengthen to a "moderate-to-strong" phase by October–December.[2,3] Whether it in fact reaches an extreme-positive state, as it did in 1997, 2006, 2019 and 2023, or instead stays moderate, closer to 2015-16, is not yet resolved by the available forecasts.

2. Exposure geography

Somalia's flood exposure is not uniform. Riverine flooding along the Shabelle (Belet Weyne, Bulo Burti, Jalalaqsi, Jowhar, Balcad, Afgooye) and the Juba (Doolow, Luuq, Baardheere, Bu'aale, Jamaame) is the largest and most predictable exposure, and it is independently corroborated by Google Flood Hub's satellite-derived inundation-history layer, which colours each 15 m pixel by how often it was under water in the historical satellite record (Sentinel-1, cross-checked against Sentinel-2) — the same river reaches recur as repeatedly inundated, not only in exceptional years.[4] A second exposure is agropastoral: FEWS NET identifies Bay, Bakool, Gedo and Hiiraan as carrying a credible Famine (IPC Phase 5) risk under extreme, widespread flooding, since these areas combine rain-fed cropping with a depleted asset base after four failed prior seasons.[5] A third exposure is northern flash flooding (Togdheer, Sool, Sanaag, Awdal, Woqooyi Galbeed, Bari), driven by short, intense local rainfall with no river-gauge network and no equivalent lead time; it falls outside both the existing gauge-based trigger and Google Flood Hub's gauge-anchored forecasts, and needs a separately calibrated rainfall-threshold trigger.

2.1 Case study: rainfall intensity and drainage capacity in Mogadishu

Mogadishu is a fourth, distinct exposure: urban pluvial flooding, driven not by river levels but by local rainfall intensity relative to an already-degraded stormwater network. Published long-run climate normals for Mogadishu range from approximately 400 to 540 mm of rain annually, depending on dataset and period.[6] On 16 September 2026, the city received approximately 5 mm of rain — not an unusual daily total — and major arterial roads, including the KM4 junction and the corridor to Aden Adde International Airport, flooded regardless, consistent with independent reporting identifying these same locations as recurring failure points since 2023.[7] On 9 May 2025, a single storm delivered 95–120 mm of rain over two to ten hours (SoDMA's own field report; a commonly cited figure attributes ~115 mm to roughly eight hours) and killed at least seven people, with some reports citing up to nine or ten as figures were updated.[8,9] That single event was on the order of twenty times 16 September's total. Read together, these two events indicate that Mogadishu's binding constraint is drainage capacity relative to short-duration rainfall intensity, not the seasonal rainfall total — a distinct risk channel from the riverine and agropastoral exposures above, and one with no equivalent early-warning lead time.

4. Why El Niño strength alone is not the right predictor

The 2015-16 and 2019 Deyr seasons are, together, a natural experiment. In 2015-16, the Pacific carried the strongest El Niño on record at the time (ONI +2.6) into the Deyr season, but the accompanying positive Indian Ocean Dipole was weak and terminated in November, before the Deyr's climatological peak — a documented contrast with 1997-98, when an extreme IOD coincided with the El Niño peak instead.[10] The result was Somalia's most contained Deyr flood season of the five examined here. In 2019, the reverse held: ONI was only weakly positive, not a conventional El Niño by most thresholds, but the IOD reached one of its most extreme positive states on record (peak DMI reported as 2.4–2.7 °C depending on dataset).[11,12] The result was 3.4 million people affected and 409,508 people displaced by flooding — comparable in scale to 2023, despite the absence of a strong El Niño.

Scaling a single reference event by an ENSO-strength ratio, as earlier drafts of this analysis did, therefore rests on the wrong variable in isolation: it would have implied 2015-16 should have been Somalia's worst Deyr flood in the 1997–2023 record, when it was in fact one of the most contained. The historical record instead groups by IOD state far more cleanly than by ONI: every event with an extreme-positive IOD produced severe displacement (60,000–418,000+); the one event with a weak/short-lived IOD did not, regardless of record-breaking ENSO.

5. Assessment for the 2026 Deyr

As of 17 September 2026, this report cannot say with confidence which side of that grouping 2026 will fall on, because the IOD has not yet resolved. Two branches are given below, read directly off the historical record in Table 1 rather than derived from a formula.

Two limitations of this table should be stated plainly rather than left for a reader to discover. First, the weak/moderate branch rests on a single historical event: one data point is a thin basis for any inference, and the "~42,000" figure should be read as "the one time this happened, it looked like this," not as a robust estimate of what a repeat would produce. Second, the death-toll bracket in the extreme-IOD branch spans nearly two orders of magnitude (29 to 1,500), driven almost entirely by the 1997-98 outlier. 1997-98 predates the gauge network, satellite-based flood forecasting and the PRMN displacement-tracking system that exist today; 2019 and 2023, both post-dating meaningful early-warning investment, cluster far lower (29 and 188). That is a reason to weight the lower end of the bracket more heavily under current preparedness capacity — but it is an analytical judgement made explicit here, not a narrowing the historical record itself supports on its own.

Forecaster language as of mid-September ("moderate-to-strong" by October–December) does not correspond exactly to either historical bucket: it is offered as stronger than 2015-16's weak/short-lived phase, but no source yet classifies it as "extreme," the term every severe historical analogue shares. The single most decision-relevant thing to monitor between now and October is therefore the weekly Dipole Mode Index, not the already near-certain ONI trajectory.

5.1 A probability-weighted expected value

Table 2 is evidence, not yet a model: it matches the forecast to a category and reads off that category's range. An expected value requires one more ingredient — a probability that the IOD resolves toward the extreme-positive branch rather than the weak/moderate one — and no forecasting centre publishes that specific number. ICPAC and the WMO give high confidence (~90 percent) that the IOD will be positive and that rainfall will be above normal across central-southern Somalia;[3,5] that is a materially weaker threshold than "extreme," and is not reused here as if it answered a different question. Rather than assert a single invented probability, Table 3 below reports the expected displacement range at three illustrative weights: a skeptical 30  percent, an agnostic 50 percent, and 80 percent — the historical base rate, since 4 of the 5 documented events in Table 1 resolved to an extreme-positive IOD.

Table 3. Expected displacement, by assumed probability of an extreme-positive IOD.
P(extreme-positive IOD)BasisExpected displacement range

This is computed as p × (extreme-branch bracket) + (1 − p) × (weak-branch bracket) for each end of the range — a genuine function of the historical brackets and an explicit weight, not a second category lookup. The same calculation cannot be honestly extended to deaths or economic loss: the weak-IOD branch (2015-16) has no verified figure for either field, and blending a populated branch with an empty one would silently treat the missing branch as zero, which this report's own tooling (analogues.weighted_expected_value()) refuses to do. For those two fields, Table 2's extreme-branch bracket remains the most defensible figure available, explicitly conditional on the IOD reaching an extreme-positive state.

5.2 Preparedness planning floor

Sections 5 and 5.1 give the best-supported evidence and the most defensible model this report can produce. For anticipatory-action sizing specifically, however, a single, conservative planning number is often more useful than a range or a probability table — provided it is clearly labelled as a sizing target rather than a forecast. This report recommends the 2023 Deyr baseline scaled by this event's ENSO strength ratio alone (1.3×, Section 2.3) as that number: it assumes, deliberately conservatively, that 2026 repeats 2023's already-extreme ocean state, with the additional margin that today's ENSO signal already exceeds 2023's regardless of how the IOD resolves.

Table 4. Preparedness planning floor: the 2023 baseline × 1.3.
BasisDeathsDisplacedDamage + losses
2023 Deyr baseline × 1.3 (this event's forecast ENSO strength ÷ 2023's) 244 543,128 $228.8M

This figure is not offered as more rigorous than Tables 2 and 3 — it is a single-analogue, ENSO-only projection, the same method this report otherwise argues against as a general-purpose estimator (Section 4). It is retained specifically because it produces a conservative, easy-to-communicate sizing target that already exceeds every historical extreme-IOD event on record (Figure 4) and every expected-value scenario in Table 3 (Figure 5): planning against it means the anticipatory response is sized for at least a repeat of 2023's severity even before accounting for how much further the IOD might push impact upward. If the IOD instead resolves toward the weak/moderate branch, this planning floor will prove to have been an overestimate — which, for a preparedness budget, is the safer direction to be wrong in.

7. Data, methods and limitations

This assessment uses a historical-analogue method: rather than fit a predictive model from first principles, it assembles every Deyr-season flood since 1997 with verifiable impact figures, classifies each by its joint ENSO/IOD state, and reads a range for 2026 directly off the analogues that share the current forecast's ocean state. The full dataset, including the exact wording of every data-quality caveat, is implemented as an open, tested module (analogues.py) rather than existing only as prose in this document — every figure in Tables 1 through 4 can be reproduced by running that module directly.

This method has real limits, stated here rather than left implicit. With five events, this is a small sample from which to generalise, and the weak-IOD branch in particular rests on a single event. Displacement figures mix sources of different precision: 2019 and 2023 use a PRMN-derived, flood-attributed figure; 2006 uses a partial, single-town figure that likely understates the full season; none of this is a like-for-like panel dataset. No economic-loss figure exists for any event except 2023, so Table 2's damage estimate for the extreme-IOD branch is a single data point presented as such, not a bracket. Finally, this report gives national, aggregate ranges only: it cannot say what share of 2026's impact, under either branch, would fall on Mogadishu specifically versus riverine towns, because no such breakdown exists in the public record for any of the five historical events either.

Appendix · Full historical event data and sourcing

1997-98 Deyr

Deaths reported as 1,500 (FAO/GIEWS) to ~2,000 (regional estimate, most in Somalia) depending on source; treat as order-of-magnitude, not a precise count. ONI reported as 2.3–2.4 °C depending on dataset version. No reliable aggregate USD damage figure was found — only sectoral physical damage (~50,000 ha farmland destroyed, 35,500 livestock lost), not converted to a USD total here. Sources: FAO/GIEWS Special Report, Eastern Africa, 5 February 1998; CARE International, El Niño 1997-98 Impacts and Response.

2006 Deyr

ONI reported only qualitatively as a "weak" El Niño (0.5–0.9 °C bracket); 0.7 used as the bracket midpoint, not a verified point figure. Displacement (60,000) covers Beledweyne specifically following the 10–11 November storms and likely understates the full-season, all-Somalia total; a separate "affected" figure of 440,000 was also reported for 2006-07 but is a different metric, not used here. No death toll found. Sources: OCHA, Somalia Floods Situation Report, 7 December 2006; FloodList.

2015-16 Deyr

The positive IOD in 2015 was weak and short-lived, peaking around September and terminating in November — before the Deyr climatological peak — unlike 1997-98. Displacement reported as 42,000 (OCHA) to 72,000 depending on source; the lower, OCHA-attributed figure is used. No direct flood-death toll was found; 84 deaths were separately reported from a cholera outbreak in flood-affected areas, a distinct causal category not counted here. Sources: OCHA flooding update, November 2015; Hong et al., J. Climate, 2017.

2019 Deyr

ONI was only weakly positive/neutral (~0.5 °C); IOD was one of the most extreme positive events on record. Peak DMI reported as 2.4 °C in some analyses and up to 2.74 °C in others depending on dataset and smoothing, with lower monthly means (~1.0 °C, Sep–Nov). Displacement (409,508) is the flood-attributed PRMN total, the same dataset used for the 2023 figure below, not a press estimate. No aggregate USD figure found; sectoral reporting cites 1,200 farms inundated and 250 livestock drowned in Gedo/Juba specifically. Sources: ACAPS Briefing Note, 4 November 2019; Lu & Ren, GRL, 2020; Ratna et al., GRL, 2021.

2023 Deyr

Deaths, damage and losses are the confirmed PDNA figures, cross-checked against a SoDMA press release ($176.0M = $126.6M direct damage + $49.5M losses; $230M recovery needs). Displacement (417,791) is the flood-attributed PRMN total; the PDNA separately reports "over 2 million displaced" using a broader, cumulative, multi-hazard definition across the full 2023 season — a different metric, not a competing estimate of the same quantity, and not used here. Sources: Government of Somalia/UN/World Bank/EU Rapid PDNA, 2024; SoDMA press release, 2024; OCHA Situation Reports No. 1–5.
