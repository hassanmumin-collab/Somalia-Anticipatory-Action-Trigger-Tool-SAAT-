Somalia's 2026 Deyr Flood Risk: Projected From the 2023 Baseline

Hassan Mumin

17 September 2026

Summary

The 2023 Deyr floods are the most recent, most thoroughly documented flood disaster in Somalia: a confirmed 188 deaths, 417,791 people displaced by flooding, and $176.0M in damage and losses. As of 17 September 2026, the current El Niño is on a forecast trajectory that already exceeds 2023's peak strength — the World Meteorological Organization's median forecast for this event's October–December peak (+2.67 °C) is 1.3× 2023's confirmed peak (+2.0 °C).

Scaling the 2023 baseline by that 1.3× ratio gives this report's central projection for the 2026 Deyr: approximately 244 deaths, 543,000 people displaced, and $228.8M in damage and losses. An exposure map covering the riverine corridor and Mogadishu's urban drainage risk (Section 2) and a set of preparedness recommendations (Section 4) follow.

1. The current forecast

For the week of 9 September 2026, the Niño 3.4 sea-surface temperature anomaly reached +2.9 °C — the highest reading of this event to date and the warmest September week in this index since 1981.[1] The World Meteorological Organization's multi-model forecast places the median October–December 2026 peak at +2.67 °C, with a 75 percent probability of exceeding the previous record set in 1982–83 and a 98 percent probability of this event reaching "Super El Niño" status.[1] The WMO Secretary-General has described the event's trajectory as potentially "off the charts."[1]

The Indian Ocean Dipole, as of 13 September 2026, stood at +0.65 °C, positive and forecast to strengthen to a "moderate-to-strong" phase by October–December.[2,3] ICPAC's seasonal outlook accordingly gives a 90 percent likelihood of wetter-than-normal conditions across central-southern Somalia for the 2026 Deyr.[3]

2. Exposure geography

Somalia's flood exposure is not uniform. Riverine flooding along the Shabelle (Belet Weyne, Bulo Burti, Jalalaqsi, Jowhar, Balcad, Afgooye) and the Juba (Doolow, Luuq, Baardheere, Bu'aale, Jamaame) is the largest and most predictable exposure, and it is independently corroborated by Google Flood Hub's satellite-derived inundation-history layer, which colours each 15 m pixel by how often it was under water in the historical satellite record (Sentinel-1, cross-checked against Sentinel-2) — the same river reaches recur as repeatedly inundated, not only in exceptional years.[4] A second exposure is agropastoral: FEWS NET identifies Bay, Bakool, Gedo and Hiiraan as carrying a credible Famine (IPC Phase 5) risk under extreme, widespread flooding, since these areas combine rain-fed cropping with a depleted asset base after four failed prior seasons.[5] A third exposure is northern flash flooding (Togdheer, Sool, Sanaag, Awdal, Woqooyi Galbeed, Bari), driven by short, intense local rainfall with no river-gauge network and no equivalent lead time; it falls outside both the existing gauge-based trigger and Google Flood Hub's gauge-anchored forecasts, and needs a separately calibrated rainfall-threshold trigger. A fourth, distinct exposure is Mogadishu itself: urban pluvial flooding, driven not by river levels but by local rainfall intensity relative to an already-degraded stormwater network, with no equivalent early-warning lead time and no river driver in common with the corridor above.

Published long-run climate normals for Mogadishu range from approximately 400 to 540 mm of rain annually, depending on dataset and period.[6] On 16 September 2026, the city received approximately 5 mm of rain — not an unusual daily total — and major arterial roads, including the KM4 junction and the corridor to Aden Adde International Airport, flooded regardless, consistent with independent reporting identifying these same locations as recurring failure points since 2023.[7] On 9 May 2025, a single storm delivered 95–120 mm of rain over two to ten hours (SoDMA's own field report; a commonly cited figure attributes ~115 mm to roughly eight hours) and killed at least seven people, with some reports citing up to nine or ten as figures were updated.[8,9] That single event was on the order of twenty times 16 September's total. Read together, these two events indicate that Mogadishu's binding constraint is drainage capacity relative to short-duration rainfall intensity, not the seasonal rainfall total.

3. The 2026 Deyr projection

This report's central projection scales the confirmed 2023 Deyr floods baseline by the ratio of this event's forecast peak ocean strength to 2023's confirmed peak: the WMO's median forecast peak Niño 3.4 anomaly for October–December 2026 (+2.67 °C) divided by 2023-24's confirmed peak (+2.0 °C), rounded to 1.3×.[1,10] The 2023 baseline itself is the confirmed toll from the joint Government of Somalia/UN/World Bank/EU Rapid Post-Disaster Needs Assessment, cross-checked against a Somali Disaster Management Agency (SoDMA) press release and OCHA situation reporting: 188 deaths, 417,791 people displaced by flooding, and $176.0M in damage and losses ($126.6M direct damage + $49.5M losses).[11]

5. Data, methods and limitations

This projection uses a single-analogue method: the 2023 Deyr floods, the most recent and most thoroughly documented flood disaster in Somalia, scaled by the ratio of this event's forecast peak ocean strength to 2023's confirmed peak. It is a deliberately simple method, implemented as an open, tested function (saat.analogues.scaled_2023_projection()) rather than existing only as prose in this document, so every figure in Table 1 and Figure 3 is reproducible by running that function directly.

Its limits should be stated plainly. It assumes flood impact scales linearly with the ONI ratio alone; it does not separately account for the Indian Ocean Dipole, antecedent soil moisture, or drainage conditions, each of which independently affects Deyr flood severity. It offers no mechanism-level or geographic decomposition: it cannot say what share of the projected impact would fall on Mogadishu specifically versus riverine towns, or what share of deaths would be attributable to drowning versus electrocution in the urban setting, because no such breakdown exists in the public record even for the 2023 baseline. The 2023 baseline's displacement figure (417,791) is the flood-attributed total from the project's PRMN dataset; the PDNA separately reports a much larger "over 2 million displaced" figure using a broader, cumulative, multi-hazard definition across the full 2023 season — a different metric, not a competing estimate of the same quantity, and not used here. Finally, this is a planning projection, not a forecast of the most likely outcome: it should be read alongside, not instead of, updates from SoDMA, FAO SWALIM and ICPAC as the season develops.

2023 Deyr baseline

Deaths, damage and losses are the confirmed figures from the joint Government of Somalia/UN/World Bank/EU Rapid Post-Disaster Needs Assessment, cross-checked directly against a Somali Disaster Management Agency (SoDMA) press release ($176.0M = $126.6M direct damage + $49.5M losses; $230M recovery needs). Displacement (417,791) is the flood-attributed total from the project's PRMN dataset; the PDNA separately reports a much larger "over 2 million displaced" figure using a broader, cumulative, multi-hazard definition across the full 2023 season — that is a different metric, not a competing estimate of the same quantity, and is not used here. Sources: Government of Somalia/UN/World Bank/EU Rapid PDNA, 2024; SoDMA press release, 2024; OCHA Situation Reports No. 1–5.

Scaling ratio

1.3× is the WMO's median forecast peak Niño 3.4 anomaly for October–December 2026 (+2.67 °C) divided by 2023-24's confirmed peak (+2.0 °C), rounded from 1.335.

Reproducing every number in this report

The 2023 baseline and the scaling function are implemented in src/saat/analogues.py (BASELINE_2023, ENSO_STRENGTH_RATIO_2026_VS_2023, scaled_2023_projection()) and covered by tests/test_analogues.py. Nothing in this report was computed only in prose.
